Sustainable packaging conversations go wrong early because three different claims get treated as one. Recycled content, recyclability and compostability describe separate properties, require separate evidence, and are not interchangeable on a pack.

A brand that prints the wrong one invites a complaint it cannot defend. Getting the vocabulary right is the first practical step.

## The three claims and what they mean

**Recycled content** describes what the packaging is made from. Post-consumer recycled fibre has been used and recovered; pre-consumer content is manufacturing offcuts. Both are legitimate, but post-consumer carries more weight and should be stated as a percentage.

**Recyclability** describes what happens after use, and it depends entirely on local infrastructure. A material is only recyclable in practice if the customer's kerbside programme accepts it. Plain corrugated and uncoated paperboard are accepted almost everywhere. Multi-layer laminated film is accepted almost nowhere.

**Compostability** describes breakdown under specific conditions. Almost all certified compostable packaging requires industrial composting at sustained high temperatures. It will not break down in a home compost bin unless it carries a separate home-compostable certification, which is much rarer.

Printing "compostable" on a pack that needs an industrial facility, in a country where most consumers have no access to one, is the single most common overclaim in packaging.

## Recycled paperboard and corrugated

This is the least complicated option and usually the cheapest to adopt.

Recycled paperboard grades are widely available and behave much like virgin board structurally. Corrugated is already made with high recycled content as standard, often above 70%, which many brands do not realise they can already claim.

The trade-offs are cosmetic. Recycled board has a warmer, greyer base tone than bleached virgin board, and print colours sit slightly differently on it. Fibre length is shorter after recycling, so very high recycled grades can be marginally less rigid at the same thickness.

For most retail packaging, none of that matters. If your brand requires a pure white base or precise colour reproduction, it might.

## Uncoated and plastic-free constructions

Removing the plastic laminate from a carton makes it straightforwardly recyclable in paper streams.

Water-based coatings and aqueous varnishes provide scuff resistance and some moisture resistance without breaking recyclability, and they have improved considerably. They do not match the barrier performance or gloss of a plastic laminate, so they are not a drop-in replacement for every application.

For grease-resistant food packaging, PE-free barrier coatings now exist and work well for short contact times. For long-contact or high-moisture applications they are still weaker than a laminate.

## Recyclable mono-material film

Flexible packaging has historically been difficult to recycle because a laminated pouch combines several polymers that cannot be separated.

Mono-material polyethylene structures solve that by building the whole pouch from one polymer family, which makes it recyclable through store drop-off programmes where those exist. Barrier performance has improved to the point where mono-PE is viable for many dry goods.

Two honest caveats. Mono-material films behave differently on filling and sealing lines — seal temperatures and dwell times usually need adjusting, so a brand switching a live product should run a trial rather than changing the whole SKU at once. And store drop-off is a much weaker recovery route than kerbside collection, so real-world recycling rates remain low.

## Certified compostable materials

PLA and PBAT-based films and coatings break down under industrial composting conditions and carry certification to prove it.

They suit closed-loop settings well — a café where cups go into a commercial composting stream, an event with controlled waste handling, a farm-to-table box scheme. In those environments the claim is real and useful.

In general retail, the picture is weaker. Industrial composting access is limited across most of the US, compostable items entering recycling streams contaminate them, and consumers routinely misunderstand the requirement. If you use compostable packaging, the on-pack wording needs to be specific about what the customer should actually do with it.

## Moulded pulp

Moulded pulp made from recycled paper is one of the better protective options. It is genuinely recyclable, often high in post-consumer content, and it replaces expanded polystyrene in most protective applications.

Its limits are dimensional tolerance and appearance. It has a rough surface and cannot hold fine detail, so it works as an internal protective layer rather than a visible presentation one.

## Reducing material is the change that actually counts

The most effective sustainability decision is usually not a material swap. It is using less.

Right-sizing a pack removes board, reduces shipping volume and cuts freight emissions in one move. A mailer trimmed by an inch across a year of orders saves more material than switching board grades, and it saves money at the same time rather than costing it.

Eliminating a component does the same. Where a fitted insert replaces loose void fill, or where a printed outer replaces an outer plus a sleeve, the pack gets simpler and lighter.

Designing for a single material stream helps too. A carton that is entirely paper recycles cleanly. The same carton with a plastic window and a foil block does not, unless the customer separates them, which they will not.

## What this costs

Recycled board is usually within a few percent of virgin equivalents, sometimes identical. Water-based coatings are broadly cost-neutral against lamination. Mono-material film carries a modest premium over conventional laminate. Certified compostable materials carry the largest premium, often 20% to 40% over conventional equivalents.

Right-sizing saves money outright, which makes it the only change on this list that improves both the environmental position and the budget.

## Making a claim you can defend

Before printing any environmental claim, be able to answer three questions: what specifically is being claimed, what evidence supports it, and what should the customer actually do with the pack.

If the answer to any of them is vague, the claim is not ready. Certification documentation, recycled content percentages and clear disposal instructions turn a marketing statement into a defensible one.

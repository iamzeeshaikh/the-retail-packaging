faqs:
  - q: What is retail-ready packaging actually required to do?
    a: Survive distribution as a case, open along a designed line without tools, and become a display that looks deliberate. Those three jobs pull against each other, which is why the format is harder than it appears.
  - q: Where do retailer requirements come from?
    a: They are usually published by the retailer and are non-negotiable — case dimensions, pallet pattern, opening face and barcode placement. Get the document before designing anything and work inward from it.
  - q: What happens if the case fails the retailer specification?
    a: It gets rejected at the distribution centre, which means the stock does not reach shelf and the run has to be remade. It is the most expensive failure in this category and it is entirely avoidable by obtaining the specification first.
  - q: How do I get the perforation right?
    a: By testing a physical sample. Too weak and it opens in transit; too strong and staff tear it badly, leaving a display that looks damaged on day one. It cannot be judged from a drawing.
  - q: Should I design for a full facing or a half-empty one?
    a: Both, but pay attention to the half-empty state, because that is how the unit spends most of its selling life. Printing the rear and side panels that become visible as it empties costs little and changes how it reads.
  - q: What stacking strength do I need?
    a: Calculated against pallet load and how long stock stands in a distribution centre, not against the contents weight alone. Compression failure appears at the bottom of a pallet, long after the specification was approved.
  - q: Can a shelf-ready case be reused or restocked?
    a: Some formats are restocked in store. If that is expected, say so — surviving repeated refilling is a different structural requirement from a single fill, and it affects both the board and the opening design.
  - q: Where does the barcode go on a retail-ready case?
    a: Wherever the retailer specifies, and never across a crease or perforation. A barcode crossing either fails at the scanner, and that is discovered in a warehouse rather than in prepress.
  - q: How far ahead should I plan a retail-ready launch?
    a: Further than a standard pack, because it adds retailer approval and physical sampling to the usual proofing. Work backwards from the on-shelf date including both, rather than from production lead time alone.
  - q: Do I need a separate primary pack and case?
    a: Almost always. The case protects and displays; the primary pack sells the individual unit. Designing them together, with the case constraints leading, avoids the common problem of a primary pack that does not fit the pallet pattern cleanly.

Retail-ready packaging, sometimes called shelf-ready packaging, is a case that arrives at a store, opens in one movement, and becomes the display without anything being unpacked. Retailers push for it because it cuts the labour of restocking. Brands benefit because it controls how the product looks on shelf rather than leaving that to whoever is filling the aisle.

The requirements are specific, and most of them have to be settled before a structural die is cut.

## The five things a retailer is judging

Retailer specifications vary, but they assess the same five properties almost universally.

**Easy identification** — staff should recognise the product and its facing from the case without reading small print. **Easy opening** — one clean action with no knife where possible, and no loose fragments. **Easy shelf placement** — the opened case fits the shelf without adjustment. **Easy shopping** — the customer can see and reach the product. **Easy disposal** — the remaining tray flat-packs or collapses cleanly.

A design that satisfies four of these and fails one usually gets rejected, because the failing one creates work in the aisle.

## Footprint comes before everything

The single most common reason a retail-ready design has to be recut is that the footprint does not match the planogram.

Shelf depth, shelf height and facing width are fixed by the retailer, and a case that is a half inch too deep does not merit a discussion — it simply does not go on the shelf. Confirm the exact planogram dimensions in writing before any structural work starts.

Pay attention to height as well as footprint. A case that fits the shelf depth but leaves too little clearance above makes restocking awkward, and retailers notice.

Where a product is sold through several retailers with different planograms, it is often cheaper to design one case to the most restrictive specification than to tool separate versions.

## Designing the opening

The opening mechanism is where most of the engineering effort goes.

A perforated tear line should open with a firm pull and stop cleanly at a defined point. Perforation strength is a genuine specification: too strong and staff resort to a knife, which damages the tray and sometimes the product; too weak and cases open in transit.

The tear should leave a tray with a front lip low enough to show the product and high enough to hold it in place. Getting that height right is a judgement about the product, not a standard figure.

Avoid designs that leave ragged edges or that require two hands and a specific technique. If a case needs explaining, it will be opened wrongly.

## Identification on the case

The case has to be identifiable while stacked, which means the printed information needs to work from the outside faces that remain visible in a pallet or a stockroom.

Product name, variant, quantity and a barcode should appear on at least two adjacent faces. Orientation arrows help where a case can be stacked the wrong way up.

Keep the case identification distinct from the consumer-facing artwork on the tray. They serve different readers — one is a stockroom aid, the other is a shelf display.

## Stacking strength is a real calculation

Cases sit under other cases on pallets and in stockrooms, and board grade has to be specified against that load rather than against the weight of the contents alone.

Compression strength depends on flute type, liner weight and how the load is stacked. A case that holds its own contents comfortably can still fail when three identical cases sit on top of it in a warm warehouse, because humidity and sustained load both reduce corrugated strength over time.

Tell your supplier the pallet configuration and the expected storage duration. Those two figures change the board recommendation more than the product weight does.

## The tray that remains

Once the case opens, the remaining tray is the display, and it is doing brand work.

Interior print matters here in a way it does not for a plain shipper. The tray walls and base are visible on shelf, and an unprinted kraft interior next to competitors with printed trays reads as an afterthought.

The tray also has to hold the product upright as units are removed. A tray that looks full and tidy at first facing but collapses inward when half the product is gone creates a messy shelf, which is exactly what retail-ready packaging is supposed to prevent.

Dividers or a shaped base solve this cheaply.

## Counter and floor units

Not all retail-ready packaging goes on a shelf. Counter display units sit at the point of payment and work to different constraints — small footprint, low height, and a header that reads from a few feet away.

Floor-standing displays are built to pallet or half-pallet dimensions and need genuine structural engineering, because they hold significant weight without a shelf supporting them. A weighted or reinforced base is usually necessary.

For both, the header card does the selling. One clear message reads from a distance; three competing ones read as none.

## Test before tooling

Build a prototype and put it through the actual sequence. Fill it with real product. Stack it as it will be palletised. Open it the way a store colleague will open it — quickly, one-handed, without reading instructions.

Then look at the tray with half the product removed. That is the state a shopper sees most often, and it is the state most designs are never checked in.

Structural dies are the expensive part of a retail-ready programme, so the prototype stage is where errors are cheap to fix. After tooling, every change costs a new die.

## Getting retailer sign-off

Where a retailer has a formal shelf-ready specification, get the design reviewed against it before production rather than after. Most large retailers will comment on a prototype, and their objections at that stage cost nothing to resolve.

Ask specifically about opening method, footprint tolerance and case identification, since those are the three that most often trigger a rejection.

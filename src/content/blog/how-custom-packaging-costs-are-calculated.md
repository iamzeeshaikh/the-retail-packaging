Packaging quotes often look opaque from the outside. Two suppliers quote the same job at prices that differ by half, and neither explains why. Understanding the cost structure makes those quotes readable, and it tells you where your own specification is spending money.

There are five cost blocks in almost every packaging quote: material, setup, printing, finishing and freight. They behave very differently as quantity changes, which is why a price that looks high at 500 units can look cheap at 10,000.

## Material is priced by area, not by box

The single most useful thing to understand is that you are buying sheet area, not boxes.

A dieline is a flat shape. The manufacturer fits as many of those shapes as possible onto a standard press sheet, and the cost of the sheet is divided by how many fit. This is why small dimensional changes sometimes produce disproportionate price changes: an extra half inch that pushes the layout from eight-up to six-up per sheet raises material cost by a third with no visible benefit.

It is also why asking a supplier whether a slightly different size would nest better is worth doing. Sometimes trimming a quarter inch costs nothing in function and saves real money.

Board grade multiplies that area cost. Moving from 14pt to 18pt is roughly a proportional increase in material spend, so it should be a decision about structural need rather than a default upgrade.

## Setup costs are fixed, and that changes everything

Plate-based printing carries a setup cost that does not change with quantity. Offset and flexographic printing need plates made for each colour. Foil stamping needs a die. Embossing needs a die. Structural die-cutting needs a cutting forme.

That fixed cost is the reason unit price falls as quantity rises. Spread a $600 plate set across 500 units and it adds $1.20 per box. Spread it across 20,000 and it adds three cents.

Digital printing has no plates, which is why it wins on short runs and loses on long ones. There is no setup to amortise, but the per-sheet running cost is higher and never falls.

The crossover generally sits somewhere between 1,000 and 3,000 units, depending on how many colours you are printing and how complex the finishing is.

## Printing cost scales with colours and coverage

On plate-based printing, each additional colour is another plate and another pass. A one-colour job is materially cheaper than a four-colour one, and a design using two spot colours can be far cheaper than a full-colour photographic design at the same quantity.

Ink coverage matters too, though less than brands expect. A design with a solid flood colour across every panel uses more ink and can require slower press speeds than a design with white space, but it is rarely the dominant cost.

Digital printing is largely indifferent to colour count, which is another reason it suits complex short-run work and versioned designs.

## Every finishing step is a separate pass

Finishing is where specifications quietly inflate. Each option below is an additional operation, often on separate equipment.

Lamination adds a pass and a film cost. Spot UV requires its own screen and pass. Foil stamping needs a die and a stamping pass. Embossing needs a die and a pass, and if you want emboss and foil registered together, that is a more demanding setup again. Windows require die-cutting the aperture and applying film. Gluing is usually included, but complex structures with multiple glue points cost more than a simple seam.

None of these are unreasonable costs. The point is that three finishes are three passes, and the price reflects that. Choosing one finish that does a clear job is usually better value than layering several.

## Tooling is a one-time cost you keep

The structural cutting die for your dieline is made once. On a first order it appears as a line item that can look startling relative to the run value. On reorders of the same size it does not appear at all, because the die already exists.

This is worth factoring into how you think about a first order. A 250-unit first run carries the full tooling cost against a small quantity, which makes the unit price look bad. The same tooling then costs nothing on every subsequent run.

It is also why changing dimensions between orders is more expensive than it appears — a new size means a new die.

## Freight is quoted separately and can surprise you

Freight is priced on weight and volume, whichever costs more. That is why flat-packed formats are cheaper to ship than pre-assembled ones, and why [rigid boxes shipped pre-formed](/custom-jewellery-boxes/) carry higher freight than folding cartons of the same finished size.

For direct-to-consumer brands there is a second freight cost that is larger than the packaging itself: the dimensional weight of every outbound parcel. Carriers charge on the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight, so an oversized mailer costs money on every single order, forever. Reducing a mailer by an inch often saves more across a year than any material change.

## What a good quote should tell you

A quote worth acting on states the board grade, the finished dimensions, the print method and colour count, the finishing operations, the quantity, the tooling position and the lead time. If it gives you a single number with no specification, you cannot compare it with anything.

It should also tell you where the price breaks are. Asking what the same job costs at the next quantity band up is one of the most useful questions a buyer can ask, because the answer often reveals that a modest increase in order size lowers the unit cost substantially.

## Practical ways to reduce cost without weakening the pack

Reduce the number of colours before you reduce the board grade. Ask whether a small dimensional change improves sheet nesting. Choose one finish instead of three. Order at a quantity that sits just above a price break rather than just below it. Keep the same dieline across product variants and change only the print, so one tooling cost serves several SKUs.

What not to cut is structural performance. The cheapest packaging in a quote is not the cheapest packaging in a year if it fails in transit. Our breakdown of [the factors that drive packaging pricing](/resources/packaging-cost-factors/) goes into where the savings are genuinely safe to take.

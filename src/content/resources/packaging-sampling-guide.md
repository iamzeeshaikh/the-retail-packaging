Sampling is the cheapest insurance in a packaging project. A sample costs a fraction of a production run and catches the failures that are otherwise discovered several thousand units too late.

The mistake is ordering the wrong kind of sample for the question you actually have.

## Three types, three questions

| Type | Tests | Typical cost | Lead time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plain structural sample | Does the product fit and the pack close | Low | 3 – 5 days |
| Printed prototype | Colour, finish, artwork position | Medium | 5 – 8 days |
| Production proof | Final approval before the run | Included or low | 1 – 3 days |

## Plain structural samples

Unprinted, cut on the actual dieline in the actual board grade. This answers the only question that matters early: does the real product fit, and does the pack close properly with it inside.

Put the actual product in the sample. Close it. Lift it. Shake it gently. Rattling means an insert is needed. A straining lid means the dieline is tight. Flimsiness with the product inside means the board grade is too light.

Order this before artwork is finished, because a structural change invalidates the artwork.

## Printed prototypes

Produced digitally on the intended substrate with your artwork applied. This answers colour and finish questions that a screen cannot.

Worth the cost when your brand depends on precise colour, when printing on kraft or uncoated stock where colour shifts, or when a finish like soft-touch or spot UV needs to be judged by hand.

Note that a digital prototype approximates an offset production run rather than matching it exactly. For critical colour, ask about a press proof instead.

## Production proofs

A digital proof confirming content, layout and position before the run. This is the final check, not a colour standard, because your screen and the press are different devices.

Check the proof against a written specification rather than from memory. Approvals given from memory are how the wrong version of a panel reaches production.

## Drop testing

For anything shipping through a carrier, load a sample with the real product and drop it from about a metre onto a hard floor, on several different faces and corners.

Not a formal protocol, but it identifies obvious failures immediately. Corners fail first, then edges, then faces.

## What sampling costs

Sample costs are normally credited against a production order that follows, which makes the real cost close to zero if you proceed.

Tooling is the exception. If a sample requires a new cutting die, that die cost carries forward to production rather than being duplicated.

## When to skip sampling

Reorders of an unchanged specification do not need new samples. A minor artwork revision on a proven structure needs a proof but not a structural sample.

Everything else — new product, new size, new material, new format — is worth sampling. Our [pre-production checklist](/resources/retail-packaging-checklist/) sets out what to verify at each stage before committing a run.

Every finish is a separate production pass with its own cost. Understanding what each one actually does prevents specifications that layer three effects competing for attention.

## Laminations

A thin plastic film bonded to the printed sheet.

| Type | Effect | Best for | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matte | Non-reflective, soft | Packs handled in store | Shows scuffing less, colour slightly muted |
| Gloss | Saturated, reflective | Vivid photography | Fingerprints visible |
| Soft-touch | Velvet texture | Premium feel | Shows marks more than matte |

Lamination adds durability and moisture resistance, but a plastic laminate makes paper packaging harder to recycle in standard streams.

## Coatings

Applied inline rather than as a separate film.

Aqueous coating is water-based, gives scuff resistance and keeps the pack recyclable as paper. It does not match lamination for gloss or barrier.

UV coating cures under ultraviolet light and gives higher gloss than aqueous. Applied as a flood or as spot UV.

Grease-resistant coatings are essential for food-contact board. PE-free versions now perform well for short contact times.

## Spot UV

High-gloss varnish applied to selected areas only. Its entire effect comes from contrast, so it works over matte lamination and is close to invisible over gloss.

Needs its own screen and pass. Register tolerance means very fine spot UV detail can shift slightly against the underlying print.

## Foil stamping

Metallic foil applied by a heated die. Requires a die, which is a one-time tooling cost.

Reproduces bold shapes cleanly and fine detail poorly, because the die crushes thin elements. Simplify the mark before stamping rather than after seeing the result. Works well on both coated and uncoated stocks.

## Embossing and debossing

Embossing raises the board; debossing recesses it. Both need a die.

Artwork must be designed for relief from the start. Applying an emboss to a logo drawn for flat print usually produces a soft, indistinct impression. Combining emboss with registered foil is achievable but a more demanding setup.

## Windows

A die-cut aperture with film applied behind it. PET is standard; PLA is available for compostable specifications, though it is less clear.

Windows weaken the panel slightly, so placement matters on structural faces.

## Cost behaviour

Finishes with dies — foil, emboss, deboss — carry a one-time tooling cost plus a per-unit pass cost. Finishes without dies — laminations, coatings — carry only the pass cost.

That means die-based finishes look expensive on short runs and reasonable at volume, while laminations cost broadly the same per unit at any quantity.

## Choosing

Ask what job the finish does: protection, contrast, texture or perceived value. If it does not have one, it is decoration you are paying a production pass for. One well-chosen finish generally reads as more considered than three competing effects.

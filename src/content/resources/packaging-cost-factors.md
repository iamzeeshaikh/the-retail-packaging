Packaging quotes are opaque from the outside. Five cost blocks explain almost all of the variation, and they behave very differently as quantity changes.

## Material is priced by area

You buy sheet area, not boxes. The manufacturer fits as many flat dielines as possible onto a press sheet, and the sheet cost divides by how many fit.

This is why small dimensional changes sometimes produce disproportionate price changes. An extra half inch that pushes the layout from eight-up to six-up raises material cost by a third with no visible benefit.

Board grade multiplies that area cost. Moving from 14pt to 18pt is roughly proportional, so it should follow structural need rather than being a default upgrade.

## Setup is fixed and does not scale

Plate-based printing needs a plate per colour. Foil, embossing and structural die-cutting each need a die. None of these costs change with quantity.

Spread a $600 plate set across 500 units and it adds $1.20 per box. Across 20,000 it adds three cents. That is the entire reason unit price falls with volume.

Digital printing has no plates, so there is nothing to amortise — but its per-sheet running cost is higher and never falls.

## Colour count and coverage

On plate-based printing, each colour is another plate and another pass. A two-spot-colour design can be far cheaper than full-colour photography at the same quantity.

Ink coverage matters less than brands expect. A flood colour uses more ink and can slow press speeds, but it is rarely the dominant cost.

Digital is largely indifferent to colour count, which is another reason it suits complex short runs.

## Finishing passes

Each finish is a separate operation, often on separate equipment.

| Finish | Cost type |
|---|---|
| Lamination | Per-unit pass plus film |
| Aqueous coating | Per-unit pass, low |
| Spot UV | Screen plus pass |
| Foil stamping | Die plus pass |
| Embossing | Die plus pass |
| Window | Die-cut plus film application |

Three finishes are three passes. One finish that does a clear job is usually better value.

## Tooling

The structural cutting die is made once. On a small first order it looks disproportionate; on every reorder at the same size it costs nothing.

This is why a 250-unit first run carries a poor unit price, and why changing dimensions between orders is more expensive than it appears.

## Freight

Priced on weight or volume, whichever is greater. Flat-packed formats ship cheaper than pre-assembled ones, which is why rigid boxes carry higher freight than folding cartons of the same finished size.

For direct-to-consumer brands there is a second, larger freight cost: dimensional weight on every outbound parcel, forever.

## Where savings are safe

- Reduce colour count before reducing board grade
- Ask whether a small dimensional change nests better on the sheet
- Choose one finish rather than three
- Order just above a price break rather than just below
- Share one structural die across several printed variants
- Right-size the pack, which cuts material and freight together

## Where savings are not safe

Structural performance. The cheapest packaging in a quote is not the cheapest packaging over a year if it fails in transit — a crushed carton costs the product, the replacement shipping and the customer.

## How the curve behaves

Packaging pricing is not linear, and understanding the shape explains most of what looks arbitrary in a quote.

Setup costs — plates, dies, press makeready, colour matching — are incurred once regardless of quantity. Running costs — board, ink, press time per unit — scale with it.

At low quantities the fixed portion dominates, so the unit price falls steeply as volume rises. Past the point where setup is spread thin, the curve flattens and further savings come only from material and press efficiency. That is why the step from 250 to 1,000 units changes the unit price dramatically and the step from 10,000 to 12,000 barely moves it.

## What each element contributes

| Element | Fixed or variable | Typical influence |
|---|---|---|
| Plates and dies | Fixed | Dominant under ~2,000 units |
| Press makeready | Fixed | Dominant under ~2,000 units |
| Board | Variable | Dominant at volume |
| Ink and coverage | Variable | Modest |
| Finishing passes | Both | One-off die plus per-unit cost |
| Sheet efficiency | Variable | Quietly significant at volume |
| Freight | Variable | Underestimated, especially outbound |

## The cost nobody quotes

For direct-to-consumer brands there is a second, larger freight cost: dimensional weight on every outbound parcel, forever.

A pack that is one size band too large costs a small amount per parcel and an enormous amount across a year of shipping. It does not appear on any packaging invoice, which is exactly why it goes unexamined.

Set against that, the difference between two board grades or two print routes is frequently trivial. The first question on any shipped product should be whether the box can get smaller.
